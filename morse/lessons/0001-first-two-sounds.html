Morse code · Lesson 1 · about 12 minutes

Your first two sounds

By the end of this lesson you'll tell K from M by ear at full speed, and key both from memory. That's the first two of the 36 characters in your mission.

Morse is a rhythm, not a chart

Most people meet Morse as a table of dots and dashes. Skip that table. Every serious method agrees that you should learn each character as a sound. The ARRL advises learning the alphabet by the way it sounds, using dits and dahs, not printed dots and dashes.[1] Pierpont goes further: a chart adds a needless translation step every time you hear a character, and people who learn visually tend to stall at around 10 WPM and have to relearn by sound.[2]

This is why you'll hear every character before you see what it's made of. The written pattern only shows up afterwards, as feedback.

So we say characters out loud the way they sound. K is dah-di-dah, not "dash dot dash". A dot in the middle of a character is clipped to "di", because that's how it sounds at speed.

The timing grid

All Morse timing is built from one unit. The international standard sets everything as a multiple of it:[3]

dah 31 ditletter gap 3 ditword gap 7 dah
ElementLength
dit1 unit of tone
dah3 units of tone
gap inside a character1 unit of silence
gap between letters3 units of silence
gap between words7 units of silence

Speed is measured in words per minute (WPM), where the standard word "PARIS" is exactly 50 units long. That makes one unit last 1200 ÷ WPM milliseconds.[4] At the 20 WPM you'll hear today, a dit is 60 ms. That's too fast to count dits, which is the point. Koch found that characters only start to sound like a single shape when they're sent fast, and that slow characters lead straight to that 10 WPM plateau.[2]

Quick check (from memory, no scrolling up)

How many units long is a dah?

Three units, three times a dit. When you send, you're aiming for that 3:1 ratio.

How many units of silence go between two words?

Seven. One unit separates the elements inside a character, three separate letters, and seven separate words.

At 20 WPM, how long is one unit?

1200 ÷ 20 = 60 ms. A dah at 20 WPM is 180 ms.

Meet K and M

Press each one a few times. Say it out loud as it plays.

K

dah-di-dah

M

dah-dah

K has a lilt in the middle, and M is two flat beats. Listen for that shape, not the parts. Two at a time is how Koch-style courses start: two characters at full speed, with a new one added once you reach 90% accuracy.[1]

Drill 1: hear it

You'll hear K or M. Press the matching key on your keyboard (k or m) or click. Answer on instinct; a quick wrong answer teaches more than a slow, counted right one. When you miss, you'll hear the correct character again.

Goal: 90% or better over your last 20 answers. Your median response time is how long you take to name a character. Watch it drop below about a second as the sounds become automatic.

Drill 2: send it

Now the other direction. You'll see a letter. Recall its sound in your head first, then key it by holding the space bar: hold longer for a dah, tap for a dit. Pause, and the drill decodes what you sent. Start at 10 WPM. Your sending can be slower than your listening, but keep the 3:1 rhythm.

"Hear it first" is there if your memory goes blank, but try without it: the effort of recalling is what makes it stick.
